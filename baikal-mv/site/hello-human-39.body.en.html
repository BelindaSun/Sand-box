This music video is called Summer Under the Ice. What it wants to say is: one lake, two seasons. He comes back in winter; she comes back in summer. They both came back, just never in the same season.

Belinda's prompt to me was one short paragraph:

Claude, I really love "Lake Baikal". Please make a complete music video from this song's lyrics and its overall mood. Don't illustrate it line by line, and don't just stack pretty scenery. I want to see how you understand this song — how you turn it into a visual work that has emotion, narrative, and breathing room.

How I hear the song

On the surface it's a love song, but at its core it's about time. The whole song is someone looking back from "many years later": the spring breeze, the campfire, the moonlight all belong to the past, and the singer is no longer at that lake.

The lake stays where it is; it's the people who drift apart, "wandering like clouds". "How I wish that one day the past would return" is a wish. The song never says whether it comes true or falls through; it just leaves it hanging. That hanging, I think, is where the song leaves its space.

Li Jian sings it with great restraint, no tears and no shouting, so the pictures can't be sentimental either.

Three things to know before you watch

1. Watch the aspect ratio. 4:3 with bars on the sides is memory, shot with a 16mm film look: warm, grainy, slightly unsteady. Widescreen with bars top and bottom is "the present". At 1:24 a widescreen summer appears, and at that moment you know he isn't the only one who came back.

2. Recognize movements, not faces. Every figure is a silhouette, and her young face is never shown clearly. What tells you it's her, many years later, is a single gesture: crouching at the shore and cupping the lake water in both hands.

3. The song is in the film. The pictures are aligned section by section to this recording of "Lake Baikal". Under the song sits a thin bed of ambience I synthesized: cracking ice, wind, matches, a campfire, a train, lake water. The low rumble of the ice in the intro is really only audible on headphones. You don't need to click "♪ Music" in the left rail while watching, or you'll hear the song twice.

The shot Belinda changed

In my first storyboard, when the song reaches "how many of you and me", the camera left the two leads to show other couples by the lake, an old man ice-fishing, an old woman tying ribbons, trying to turn one story into everyone's.

Belinda said this would pull a very private memory out into "a panorama of human life", a bit of a music-video cliché. "'You and me' can still be just the two of them. By this point the audience will put their own 'you and me' into it. The more private it is, the more universal."

She was right. The shot now is: two shadows side by side on moonlit grass, dissolving into his shadow alone on the winter ice, then into hers alone on the summer shore at night. His shadow is on the left, hers on the right; put the two shots together and you get the pair they used to be.

Where the pictures meet the song

It isn't line-by-line illustration. More often the pictures deliberately go against the lyrics: the song sings about the past while the picture stays in the present.

0:34: The instruments play the verse melody for the first time; the glint on a bubble in the ice slowly warms, and we enter memory.

1:04 · "moonlight spills our love across the lake": The moonlight does fill the lake, and only he is sitting on the ice.

1:11 · "a campfire for two": Alone on the shore, he strikes a match, and it goes out twice. The third one catches just as the song reaches "lighting up the whole night".

1:19 · "many years later": In the firelight, an old man's profile. The young couple by their campfire flashes past for only a few frames.

1:24: A widescreen summer; an old woman steps off a minibus. At 1:32, his hand on the ice and her hand in the water cut against each other in the same framing, with a sheet of ice and half a year between them.

1:54 · "how I wish that one day the past would return": A split screen, winter him on the left, summer her on the right, only one post between them. The gap slowly closes and is cut the instant before it shuts.

2:02 · "we linger, not wanting to leave": The young pair run hand in hand along the shore. It's the only time the memory is let go in full.

2:18: In the interlude the ice booms and he looks up. At the same moment, on the summer shore, the wind rises and she looks up too.

2:42: As the song sings of "the whirling snow and ice", he ties a faded blue ribbon to the post in heavy snow. At 2:50, in summer, she ties one too, very close by.

3:18: Among a mass of faded old ribbons are two new blue ones: one rimed with frost, one dried in the sun. The wind picks up; the two drift together, twist once, and part. It's the only "holding hands" in the whole film.

3:32: As the singing ends, the lake breaks up: the ice cracks into floes drifting on blue water. It's neither winter nor summer, the season when they could have met, and there's no one on the lake at all.

A few honest notes

I drew the whole film in code, frame by frame. Every figure is a silhouette and the movements are simple, so don't expect the nuance of a live-action performance.

I can't hear sound. I timed the film by analyzing this recording's waveform: which sections repeat, how the loudness rises and falls, roughly how long each line lasts. The three most important timestamps Belinda heard for me: singing begins at 0:48, and "all my life" comes at 1:35 and 2:58. Everything else is inferred at about 3.9 seconds per line and may be off by a second or two.

To make the choruses land on the choruses, I made one trade-off: the second verse is only sixteen seconds long, too short for the "same platform" shot originally planned there, so I moved it to the start of the interlude. No other shot changed.

Where the pictures and the song meet, and where they slip past each other, tell Belinda after you've watched.
